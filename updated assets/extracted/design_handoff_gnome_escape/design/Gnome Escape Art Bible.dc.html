Art direction · Look and feel

The room is a silhouette. Colour means you can touch it.

One rule governs everything else in this document. The ruins are lit from behind: the background is a bright limestone haze, and every piece of foreground geometry is a near-black ink silhouette against it. Nothing in the foreground carries colour except the things a gnome can interact with, the hazards that can kill them, and the gnomes themselves.

This is not a style choice first. It is a legibility choice. Two players are watching one screen and reading it in a hurry; a room built this way tells them instantly which pixels matter. It also makes the art cheap — silhouetted stone needs no interior detail, so the budget goes into the eight things that move.

Departure from current art

The existing reference is mossy grey stone rendered in full detail, with the gnomes in grey and green. That look has a specific problem: the stone, the moss, the fan and the gnomes all sit in the same mid-value range, so a screenshot reads as texture rather than as a puzzle. This direction keeps the enchanted-ruins setting and throws out the value structure. Stone Gnome becomes warm sandstone, Wind Gnome becomes mint — the two gnomes now sit at opposite ends of the hue wheel and can never be confused at speed.

Rules

  • Foreground geometry uses ink only. If a wall needs to read as different material, change its silhouette, not its colour.
  • Gold means a gnome can act on it: levers, plates, gates, blocks in their pushable state, exits. Nothing decorative is ever gold.
  • Cyan means water and only water. Violet means moving air and only moving air.
  • The two gnome pairs are reserved. No tile, particle or UI element may use them.
  • Haze steps may shift hue per room (see section 6). Ink and signal colours never shift.

5 · Lighting

Light comes from behind and above the room, off-screen. There is no light source in the scene, so nothing casts a directional shadow and no tile needs a lit side and a dark side. This is what makes the tileset small.

  • Three haze planes. Brightest at the top of the frame, mid at eye level, recess at the bottom. Hard horizontal bands, no gradient dithering — the bands are structure, not atmosphere.
  • Parallax. The haze planes do not scroll; rooms are single-screen. The only depth cue is value, and that is enough.
  • Rim light. Every foreground silhouette gets a 4 px top facet one ink step lighter. That single edge is the whole lighting model.
  • Contact shadow. Gnomes and blocks get a 2 px ink ellipse under them at 40% alpha. Nothing else does.
  • Signal glow. Gold and violet elements bleed one pixel of their light step into the surrounding haze. Cyan does not — water absorbs.

The trap to avoid. The temptation with a backlit scene is to add interior detail to the silhouettes so they look less flat — brick courses, moss, cracks in mid ink. Every step in that direction costs legibility and gains nothing, because the player is not looking at the walls. Detail belongs in the haze planes, where it cannot compete with the signal colours.

6 · Six rooms, one tileset

The rooms differentiate by rotating the four haze steps around the hue wheel and leaving everything else alone. One tileset, one sprite set, six distinct rooms. Room 6 is the only inversion — the haze goes dark, the silhouettes read as lighter than their background for the first time, and the game says out loud that this is the last one.

Implement as a per-room palette swap on the haze channel only, not as six sets of tiles. In practice that is four hex values per room in the level data.

7 · The moment it happens

Co-op needs loud feedback. Two players are not looking at the same part of the screen, so every state change has to announce itself where it happens and be audible to someone looking elsewhere.

ResetWipe from both screen edges inward over 10 frames. Never a fade — a fade reads as a bug.Short reversed whoosh
One gnome in a doorDoor glow goes steady, a gold pip lights on that gnome's HUD tagSingle held note, one of two in a chord
Both in — solvedThe two notes resolve, gold floods outward from both doors, silhouettes lift toward the haze for 30 framesThe chord completes. This is the game's one moment of real payoff; spend on it.

The two-note rule. Each exit door owns one note of a two-note chord. A player standing in their door alone hears their note held, unresolved, which is the clearest possible instruction to the other player without a line of text. Everything else in the audio design can be cheap; this cannot.

8 · The shell

Four screens, all of them in the same 480×288 buffer and the same palette as the game. The shell never switches to a different visual language — ink silhouette, haze ground, gold for anything you can select.

HUD

There is almost none, and that is the point. Two tags in the top corners, 20 px tall, each carrying its gnome's colour swatch, its control scheme and a door pip that lights when that gnome is in position. Nothing else — no timer, no counter, no ability icons. If a mechanic needs a HUD element to be understood, the mechanic needs redesigning.

Open

  • Room 6's inversion is the boldest call here. It costs one extra palette state and it is the only place the rule bends. Worth a look before it gets built.
  • The 5×7 bitmap font is a real build cost. If it needs to go, the fallback is a monospace web font at exactly 8 or 16 px — never scaled between.
  • Canvas moves from 960×560 to 960×576. Small change, but it touches every hard-coded Y value in the current level.
  • No sprite art exists in this direction yet. What is here is a spec and a set of diagrams, not finished pixels — the palette, sizes and read rules are the deliverable.
